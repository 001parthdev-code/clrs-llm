The FFT

The fast Fourier transform (FFT) takes advantage of the special
properties of the complex roots of unity to compute DFTn(a) in (n lg
n) time, as opposed to the (n2) time of the straightforward method.
Assume throughout that n is an exact power of 2. Although strategies

for dealing with sizes that are not exact powers of 2 are known, they are
beyond the scope of this book.

The FFT method employs a divide-and-conquer strategy, using the

even-indexed and odd-indexed coefficients of A(x) separately to define
the two new polynomials Aeven(x) and Aodd(x) of degree-bound n/2:

Interpolation at the complex roots of unity

coefficient form to point-value form by evaluating the polynomial at the
complex roots of unity, pointwise multiplying, and finally converting
from point-value form back to coefficient form by interpolating. We've
just seen how to evaluate, so now we'll see how to interpolate the
complex roots of unity by a polynomial. To interpolate, we'll write the
DFT as a matrix equation and then look at the form of the matrix
inverse.
